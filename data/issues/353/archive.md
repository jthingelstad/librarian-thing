## Notable

### [Introducing Claude Haiku 5.5 Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-haiku-5-5)

I've been hoping to see a new version of Haiku for a long time and it is finally here. I moved a handful of projects to it the night it was released. With all the focus being on the most capable models, why care about Haiku? 

When you are building agentic workloads you inevitably have various tasks that you need to deliver **very cheaply** and Haiku is the workhorse behind much of that. I probably run 100 times more Haiku calls compared to Sonnet and Opus. So the volume is there and you need this to work really well. 

Additionally because Haiku was so old it didn't have the effort and other modern capabilities of the newest models. That is now fixed so developers can rely on the same mechanics and work across various Claude models with ease. 👏

Also see [Simon Willison's post on Haiku 5.5](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/claude-haiku-5-5/).

### [We’re going to need default hard budget caps on pretty much everything](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/3/default-hard-budget-caps/)

I read this article with a smile as over the last few months I've added this as a feature to nearly every agentic workload that I run.

> Here’s a product feature which the world is going to need a whole lot more of over the coming months and years: **default hard budget caps**. I’m talking about the feature of pay-by-usage services and APIs that lets you say “after $X/month, cut this thing off and return errors”. These need to be **hard** limits. Soft caps, “after $X/month, send me a warning email”, will not cut it.

By the way you can do this with the agentic loop having awareness. One of the agent projects I've been working on gets a hard monthly spend limit. From that limit it derives a budget and determines each day how it is pacing to that budget. So, not only can you make these systems cost-controlled but you can hand over part of the challenge of managing that to the system itself. 

We definitely need this on the low end of the market for small and personal projects, however it is just as badly needed at the enterprise side. I can't tell you how many vendors I've seen that move to credit models and they rather conveniently have no way to allow you to hard-cap the spend. This forces you as the customer to have to keep track or end up on the wrong side of a negotiation after you've burned through credits.

### [Introducing zkAPI: private usage credits for any API](https://blog.ethereum.org/2026/10/01/introducing-zkapi)

I've been suggesting that crypto is a better way for agents to handle payment for services for a bit. Here is a broader example of generally using crypto to pay for service usage without having to divulge your identity with it.

> [zkAPI](https://ethresear.ch/t/zk-api-usage-credits-llms-and-beyond/24104) separates payment from identity. You deposit credits (ETH, USDC, etc.) into a vault contract on Ethereum, one ordinary transaction. From then on, your balance exists as a private note: digital cash that only you can spend and that nobody can trace back to the deposit.
> 
> To authorize spending, software on your machine produces a small zero-knowledge proof that says, in effect, that a funded note covers this spend and nobody has spent it before. One proof can cover a single request or a whole session. The server can check that the statement is true without learning which note, which deposit, or which person. At the payment layer, requests do not link to you, and they do not link to each other.

I'm glad that folks are thinking about this. The speed of the web shouldn't be limited by the pace of bank settlements and we shouldn't have to forego our privacy in exchange for simply using an API.

### [On the Rumored M8 Ultra AI Servers](https://www.macsparky.com/blog/2026/10/on-the-rumored-m8-ultra-ai-servers/)

I keep thinking about the strategic advantage that Apple finds itself in with the M-series chips and AI.

> If an M8 Ultra server carries forward the unified-memory architecture and performance-per-watt advantages of Apple’s current chips, it could be an interesting machine for private AI inference. Its job would be narrower than replacing Nvidia across the industry. Instead, it gives buyers another credible option for private workloads that demand local control without wasting power.

It is unique that Apple can put enough compute power in a box that can sit on a desk, be powered with a regular outlet, not require special cooling, and be quiet enough to not need to be in a room of its own. For a number of professions and use cases with AI I also suggest we are going to want to go **opposite** of the last 20 years of cloud migration and run that workload locally. The most precise cost management we can have is running something on our own hardware. And if you are dealing with sensitive data running it locally is a great way to protect it. 

Apple could be a big player in this space because of their unique advantage in compute power per watt.

### [Tufte's Razor · An interactive guide to the data-ink ratio](https://tuftesrazor.scienceux.org/)

I've gone to Tufte's data visualization seminars multiple times and always walk away with the general concept that some ways of visualizing numbers are just used to fill space. Have 5 numbers but need to fill a whole slide? Pie charts to the rescue. 

This is a really well done interactive explorer that allows you to tweak things and see the impact.

The list of 11 checks for any visualization is a good review:

> 1. The title says what the chart shows.
> 2. Every number has its unit.
> 3. Each value is said once.
> 4. Nothing is shaded, boxed or outlined without a reason.
> 5. Bars fill about half to two-thirds of their space.
> 6. Bars start at zero.
> 7. Labels sit next to what they name.
> 8. Long names go on horizontal bars.
> 9. Categories are sorted, unless they have an order of their own.
> 10. The one thing that matters stands out.
> 11. Every word is readable at arm's length.

Help bring better charts to the world.
